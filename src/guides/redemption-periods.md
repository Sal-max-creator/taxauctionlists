---
title: Redemption periods and what they mean for you
description: How long owners have to pay you back, and why that clock matters for liens and deeds.
order: 6
---

The redemption period is how long the owner has to pay off the debt after a tax sale. It's set by state law, and it's the single biggest factor in how long your money is tied up.

## For lien buyers

During the redemption period, the owner can redeem at any time by paying the county what's owed plus interest or a penalty, and the county passes it to you. Short redemption periods return money faster. Long ones lock it up longer, but often at a set rate.

Some states have rules that change your return, like a minimum penalty if the owner redeems early, or interest that changes over time. Each [state page](/states/) explains the basics and links to the official rules.

## For deed buyers

In many deed states, the sale is final once the county issues the deed. In redeemable deed states, the former owner can still buy the property back for a period after the sale by repaying your purchase price plus a penalty. During that time you may not be able to sell, and often shouldn't make big improvements.

## What starts and stops the clock

The period usually starts on the sale date, but some states count from when the certificate is issued or from a later notice. Bankruptcy can pause it. Read the statute or the county's terms, and put the deadline on your calendar the day you buy.

## When the period ends

If a lien isn't redeemed, you usually have a limited window to apply for a deed or start foreclosure, often with notice requirements to the owner and other parties. If you miss it, the certificate can expire and you can lose your investment.
